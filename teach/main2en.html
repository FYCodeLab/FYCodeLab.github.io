Understanding artificial intelligence

From a straight line to a neural network

Begin with a familiar biology experiment and discover, step by step, how a very simple model can become artificial intelligence.

1

Regression is prediction

As biologists, you have probably already prepared a calibration curve by plotting optical density (OD) against concentration. You measured several standards, placed the results on a graph, and drew the straight line that passed as close as possible to the experimental points.

You were performing linear regression.

A regression line is more than a visual summary of your results: it is also a simple predictive model. Once the line has been fitted, a new OD measurement can be used to estimate the concentration of an unknown sample.

y = ax + b
  • x is the input, such as concentration.
  • y is the predicted output, such as OD.
  • a controls the slope of the line.
  • b controls where the line crosses the vertical axis.

In a calibration experiment, the model technically predicts OD from concentration. We can then use the same relationship in reverse to estimate an unknown concentration from its measured OD.

2

A line can behave like an artificial neuron

You can move the line by changing a and b in the equation y = ax + b. Adjusting two parameters seems quite simple. Yet this small calculation already contains the fundamental idea of an artificial neuron.

In the 1940s and 1950s, researchers began developing mathematical models inspired by biological neurons. Information enters the artificial neuron, is transformed using adjustable parameters, and then produces an output.

Input x  →  calculation ax + b  →  output y

The parameter a determines how strongly the input affects the result. In artificial intelligence, it is usually called a weight. The parameter b is called the bias. Together, the weight and bias determine the neuron’s prediction.

In this example, the regression line therefore behaves like a very simple artificial neuron. Changing a and b changes its behaviour.

A biologist’s note: an artificial neuron is only very loosely inspired by a biological neuron. Real neurons have structures, electrical properties and biochemical interactions that are far more complex.

3

Adding variables

So far, our prediction has used only one input variable, x. But what happens if the result depends on two variables?

Imagine that we want to predict a biological response using both the concentration of a compound and the duration of exposure. The model now receives two inputs:

y = a1x1 + a2x2 + b
  • x1 and x2 are the two input variables.
  • a1 and a2 are their respective weights.
  • b is the bias.
  • y is the model’s prediction.

With one input, the model can be represented by a line. With two inputs, it becomes a plane in three-dimensional space. The goal remains the same: adjust the parameters until the plane passes as close as possible to the experimental points.

Activity 3

Fit a plane in 3D

This time, fitting the model by eye is much more difficult. Change the parameters to bring the plane closer to the points.

A model can use far more than two variables: 4, 20 or even 200. We cannot easily represent such a model because our brains are comfortable visualising no more than three dimensions. For an artificial neuron, however, the principle remains the same: each input has its own adjustable weight.

The real difficulty is no longer writing the equation, but finding the correct values for all those parameters.

4

What are artificial neurons used for?

One artificial neuron can make only a relatively simple prediction. However, many artificial neurons can be connected to form a neural network. By passing information from one neuron to another, the network can combine a very large number of inputs and adjustable parameters. This allows it to learn much more complex relationships.

Consider an image-classification task. We might give the network images belonging to two classes—for example, photographs of your face and photographs of your neighbour’s face.

A computer does not initially see a face in the way that we do. The image must first be converted into numerical information. A process called embedding represents the important features of an image as a list of numbers. These numbers become the inputs of the neural network.

The information then travels through the network. Each artificial neuron performs a small calculation using its weights and bias. At the end, the network produces a score for each possible class. The highest score represents the network’s prediction.

At first, the network’s parameters are not correctly adjusted, so its predictions are poor. During training, the network compares each prediction with the correct answer and measures its error.

A method called backpropagation then works backwards through the network to determine how the parameters should be modified to reduce that error. The network repeats this process many times. Each complete pass through the training data is called an epoch.

Once the parameters have been adjusted, their final values define your trained model.

In the first activity, you trained a model manually by drawing and adjusting a line. In this final activity, the parameters were adjusted automatically using backpropagation. The network is more complex, but the principle is the same: find the parameter values that allow a mathematical model to make good predictions.

The trained model can now be tested using images it has never seen before. It can even be downloaded and used on another computer.

Congratulations—you have trained your first AI model!
